01 Background

As of 2024, 2.6 billion people—about one-third of the world’s population—remain offline, and
internet use ranges from over 90% in high-income countries to just 27% in low-income
countries. Meanwhile, 800 million people still lack official ID, limiting access to digital public
services, finance, and healthcare. Closing that divide is the mandate of the World Bank
Group's Digital & AI Vice Presidency, which supports countries to expand affordable,
inclusive broadband connectivity, bring people onto digitally enabled services, and bring
more women online, commitments tracked on the World Bank Group Scorecard.

Employment is the second challenge. Over the next decade, an estimated 1.2 billion young
people in the Global South will reach working age, while current trends are projected to
generate only 420 million jobs.

Artificial intelligence and the broader data revolution are expanding what institutions can do,
improving service delivery, and opening new economic pathways. Yet not everyone is
benefiting equally. This challenge sits inside that agenda. Most AI applications have been
deployed and designed with the assumption of baseline access to reliable connectivity, use
of a smart-model device, and a user already familiar with how to prompt when interacting
with an AI interface. This challenge is designed for the many settings where these conditions
are either strained, limited, or intermittent.


02 What is Small AI

Small AI is a design approach for AI development that promotes targeted, use-case-driven
AI: eg. while general-purpose large language models continue to improve their viability in
constrained environments, a Small AI approach focuses on leveraging AI capabilities —
pattern recognition, text generation, or the ability to understand context1 — to work on a
single, well-defined problem, on a device easily accessible to users. That device may be a
basic phone, or a small piece of on-device hardware such as a sensor or a microcontroller
that runs the model locally. The AI use case may be predictive, generative, or agentic; what
matters is that its targeted use, less reliance on the existence of an extensive data-center
infrastructure, functions where connectivity is weak or absent, can be trained or tuned on
local data, and centers the user through common interfaces such as SMS and voice. The
design is governed by the operating constraint and the problem to be solved, not by the
scale or capability of the model.

This comes with an important caveat. A good AI solution is not sufficient on its own. Small AI
depends on a set of foundational enablers — adequate connectivity, digital literacy, and
trusted local institutions — and without them even the best tool remains a pilot. Each sector
brief below therefore sets out these preconditions alongside the challenges specific to the
sector, so that a proposed AI solution can demonstrate a clear and distinct value add.
Applicants should address one sectoral challenge to build their Small AI solution.

03 What is the Small AI for Development Hackathon?

In this regard, the World Bank Group Digital & AI Vice Presidency, in collaboration with the
Youth Summit, Development Economics Vice Presidency (DEC), Information and
Technology Solutions (ITS) and Hack-nation, is organizing a hackathon to bring the youth
perspective into how AI is developed. Building on the World Bank Group's work to help
countries adopt and scale AI, including the AI Repository, a catalogue of AI use cases and
the lessons from deploying them. Over one weekend, applicants will build a working Small AI
solution to one of the sector challenges below.

Through their Small AI solution, applicants should seek to answer: what does localizing AI
development mean for you? Young people are often closest to the challenges this
competition addresses, and their perspective on how technology should respond is
essential. This challenge invites young people to consider what it means to develop AI for
their own context, language, and local needs, and to reflect candidly on both the
opportunities and the risks. Entries that acknowledge these trade-offs are encouraged.

The competition is open to entrants aged 18 to 35, in line with the United Nations’ definition
of youth, open to participants from over 150 countries. Applicants from emerging economies
are encouraged to apply.

   •   Application Opens: September 2026

   •   Shortlist selection by expert evaluators: 5–6 October
   •   Ignite Talk by the winners in Seoul: 21 October 2026


Entries submitted during the competition weekend will be reviewed by expert evaluators
drawn from health, agriculture, and tourism, who will select the shortlist for final judging.

chosen per sector, three winners in total.

One representative from each of the three winning teams, one from each sector, will be
invited to Seoul to present at the Global AI and Digital Summit 2026, co-hosted by the World

governments, the private sector, investors, and international organizations to examine how

economies, with dedicated attention to health, agriculture, tourism, education, and
government services. The Ignite Talks will take place in a breakout session. Organizers will
facilitate networking opportunities and, where feasible, support introductions to investors,
private-sector representatives, MSMEs, and government officials attending the event. Travel
expenses will be covered. Visa arrangements will be communicated to each winning team
directly (one representative per team will be traveling).

05 The Challenge

Who you are building for
Over one weekend, you build one Small AI tool/solution for one sector and demonstrate it
works within the constraints specified within each sector. Each sector's scenario, limits,
evidence, and datasets are in its annex. (Annex A: Health, Annex B: Agriculture, Annex C:
Tourism)

Meet Noor
Noor is 38, farms 2 hectares in the Ondera highlands; coffee on the upper slope, maize and
beans below and has been a member of the Ondera Coffee Cooperative for eleven years.
Her 16-year-old daughter boards at school in the district town. At home she speaks her local
language, and the national one when she needs it like most of her neighbors do when they
have to interact with tourists every now and then. Two phones serve the household: her
own, which she uses for calls, messages, and mobile money, and her daughter's
smartphone, which she only really uses when her daughter is home on weekends to set it up
and show her how, most often to watch the news on YouTube. There is no Wi-Fi at the
house; they buy 3G data bundles from the local network when they need them. For most of
the day she is out on the slope, and the phone is at the house.

06 The Rules

Be creative, think out of the box, whatever form your solution takes:

    •    it runs on a device the user already has;
    •    its core feature works offline;
    •    its model files are small enough to side-load or send over a weak connection;
    •    at least one interaction is in a local language, by voice or text — name the language,
         and expect to be asked how the tool would fare in a less-supported one.


    AI Guardrails
    — Human-in-the-loop principles: A person makes the final call. The tool informs a decision
    and flags what it is unsure of; it does not act on the user's behalf. If an entry proposes an
    agentic workflow, ensure the AI checks in with user appropriately.
    — Avoid hallucinations.

7.2 The two kinds of data

1. Data that shows the problem and gap being filled by the solution. Surface data that
puts Noor's situation in context — health-worker absence rates, travel time to the nearest
facility, the gap between phone and smartphone ownership among women. Cite source,
year, and country. Make note if the closest figures come from synthetic data modeling an
article or published journal

2. Data you build with. What your tool learns from: speech recordings, labeled
photographs, example messages. Name every dataset, its source, its license and its size.
You must also indicate what your data does not cover, and this is scored. Most crop-
disease datasets, for instance, are studio images on plain backgrounds; a model trained only
on them performs poorly on real field photos. You may supplement with synthetic data as
long as you label it accordingly.

Annex A. Health

A.1 The scenario
Noor has a clinic near where she lives, but it is overcrowded and the quality of care is not
always of a good standard. Doctors are well-meaning but are not always up to date with the
latest medical guidance. The significant patient load, coupled with burdensome record-
keeping requirements, means that they are not able to give each patient the attention that is
needed.

   •   The problem. Access to primary care in remote catchments is limited by clinician
       scarcity and the absence of on-site diagnostics. Patients travel long distances to be
       seen, health workers have limited time per case, and low patient digital literacy
       compounds both.
   •   What other digital tools have been in use. Basic mobile technology has delivered
       measurable health gains so far. The Mwana project in Zambia and Malawi ran
       entirely on SMS: built on the RapidSMS platform, it transmitted infant HIV test results
       from central laboratories back to rural clinics and sent automated appointment
       reminders to mothers, cutting result turnaround times sharply. Uganda's mTrac used
       the same SMS foundation to collect weekly data from health facilities into the national
       health information system and to push medicine stock-out alerts, reaching over 10
       million subscribers. Both worked through message routing and data aggregation. The
       Broadband Commission estimated that wider mHealth adoption in Sub-Saharan
       Africa could save approximately one million lives over five years against malaria,
       HIV/AIDS, and perinatal conditions.
   •   Where AI can add value. The clinical use of AI in low-resource health with the
       strongest evidence base is image-based screening. In 2021, the World Health
       Organization endorsed computer-aided detection (CAD) software for chest X-ray
       interpretation in tuberculosis screening in individuals aged 15 and older, based on
       the evaluation of three products — CAD4TB, Lunit INSIGHT CXR, and qXR — that
       performed comparably to, and in later versions better than, human readers. These
       tools are now deployed in active case-finding programmes across India, the
       Philippines, and some countries in Africa, often paired with ultra-portable X-ray
       hardware. AI-enabled retinal screening for diabetic retinopathy and visual inspection
       tools for cervical pre-cancer follow the same pattern: the model reads an image and
       flags the cases that warrant a clinician's review.
   •   The risks. The World Health Organization (WHO)'s Ethics and governance of
       artificial intelligence for health is explicit that opportunities and risks are linked, and
       cautions against unethical collection of health data, biases encoded in algorithms,
       and risks to patient safety and cybersecurity. Models trained largely on data from
       high-income countries can underperform on the populations they are then deployed
       to. Bias can be encoded silently and reproduced across many patients before it is
       detected. Patient data moving through a mobile network raises safety exposures that
       a paper record does not. A confident wrong output where a life is at stake is worse
       than no output. Applicants should design for these risks from the start.
   •   Preconditions to keep in mind. International Finance Corporation (IFC)'s
       TechEmerge Health program covered Kenya, Uganda, and Ethiopia. Even its
       strongest AI-assisted ECG and diagnostic tools needed 2G/3G to work. Clinician
       trust and regulatory acceptance took years to build before anything scaled. Health

Annex B. Agriculture
B1. Scenario

Noor’s coffee yields have dropped this season, and she cannot say why. The nearest
extension officer visits the sub-county twice a year at best, and at harvest she sells her
parchment to whichever middleman drives up the valley, at whatever price he names.

    •   The problem. Smallholder farmers lack timely, localized advice on what is affecting
        their crop, and they lack independent information on what that crop is worth when it is
        time to sell. Public extension services are constrained by staff shortages, manual
        data collection, and delayed alerts. In many settings, the binding constraint is the
        absence of a working farmer registry rather than the absence of an algorithm; without
        a registry, farmers cannot be reached at scale by any tool, digital or otherwise.
    •   What other digital tools have been in use. In Côte d'Ivoire, the World Bank has
        supported digital platforms that connect farmers to advisory content and to markets,
        registering producers and delivering price and input information through the web and
        mobile channels. The World Bank Group's AgriConnect initiative aims to reach 300
        million farmers and double annual agribusiness investment to $9 billion by 2030.
    •   Where AI can add value. India's Wadhwani AI cotton pest tool uses computer
        vision: a farmer photographs a pheromone trap, and the model counts and identifies
        the pests on the image, then advises whether and when to spray. That is pattern
        recognition across thousands of visual examples, compressed to run on a basic
        smartphone offline. Voice-based advisory in local languages is a second line of
        promising work, particularly where literacy or screen literacy is a constraint.
    •   Preconditions to keep in mind. In Ukraine, a working farmer registry unlocked
        advisory services, insurance, and grants for 150,000 farmers. AI stacked on absent
        registries, absent phones, or absent trust in the advisory system will be difficult to
        implement in practice.


    Hackathon challenge
    Design and demonstrate a Small AI solution that helps Noor make, communicate or act on
    one better agricultural decision; for example identifying a crop or post-harvest problem,
    timing a farming activity, accessing localized advisory, documenting a field observation,
    improving quality, value addition or connecting evidence to a pricing, market or extension-
    service next step.

Annex C. Tourism
C.1 The scenario
Noor's farm is not marketed on a digital platform. The visitors who find it arrive and have
help translating through a local guide, and she has no way of knowing what value she
created, what worked during the tourists’ visits and what did not once they leave.

    •   The problem. Tourism is a major job creator in the economies where Noor lives, and
        small operators are where most of that employment sits. Their ability to hire and grow
        depends on customers they can reach and on understanding the value those
        customers actually take home. Most small operators run on instinct: they know
        visitors leave happy, but not why, not which parts of the experience are worth
        building on, and not how to turn a good review into a returning customer, a referral,
        or a new product. Weak digital presence and marketing that rarely extends past word
        of mouth compound the gap.
    •   What other digital tools have been in use. In Jordan, the World Bank has
        supported work to bring youth and small operators onto digital platforms and into
        online services, including training operators to list and manage their business on
        booking and marketing platforms. Getting an operator visible online with a listing, a
        booking page, digital payments, already unlocks customers without any AI.
    •   Where AI can add value. Small language models can read across a scattered set of
        reviews and messages and tell the operator what visitors keep returning to, what they
        wish had been different, and which parts of the experience are worth building the
        next product around; the analytical work most small operators cannot do for
        themselves.
    •   Pre-conditions to keep in mind. In Jordan, the binding constraint for informal
        operators was the absence of a digital listing and the skills to manage one.


    Hackathon challenge
    Design and demonstrate a Small AI solution that helps Noor, as a small tourism operator,
    complete one meaningful business workflow; for example, becoming discoverable,
    communicating across languages, responding to enquiries, shaping an experience,
    managing a booking, learning from vistor feedback or following up with a guest.
